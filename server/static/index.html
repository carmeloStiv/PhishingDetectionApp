      How a verdict is reached

      No single technique detects phishing reliably. This tool combines four kinds of evidence, keeps them visibly separate, and never lets a guess masquerade as a fact.

      Threat intelligence

      Lookups against lists of addresses already confirmed as malicious (the OpenPhish feed, and Google Safe Browsing, VirusTotal and URLhaus when the operator has configured keys). A match is the strongest evidence there is. No match proves nothing: new phishing pages take hours to be listed.

      Rule Page content

      Deterministic checks for known tricks: misspelt and look-alike brand names, a real domain used as a decoy sub-domain, hidden destinations, raw IP addresses, password forms that send data elsewhere, and in messages the combination of who claims to write, what is asked and where the links go.

      ML model

      A gradient-boosted model trained on tens of thousands of phishing and legitimate addresses scores how much an address resembles phishing. It is a statistical hint. On its own it can make a result “Suspicious”, never “Likely Phishing”, and it is ignored for well-known domains.

      Reputation

      Whether the domain is among the most-visited sites (Chrome UX Report), an official domain of a known organisation, and how long ago it was registered. Reputation supports a “Likely Safe” verdict but never overrides a threat-intelligence match.

      The risk score

      Each risk indicator has a fixed weight set by its severity: critical 0.85, high 0.55, medium 0.30, low 0.12. Weights are combined as score = 100 × (1 − ∏(1 − weight)). Independent findings reinforce one another, one weak signal cannot produce a high score, and indicators describing the same fact are counted once. The score measures the strength of the evidence found. It is not the probability that something is malicious.

      The four verdicts

      Likely Phishing
      A confirmed threat-intelligence match, or a score of 65 or more from several significant indicators.
      Suspicious
      Score between 30 and 64. Warning signs exist, but not enough for a firm conclusion.
      Likely Safe
      Score below 30 and positive evidence, such as an official or long-established domain, or clean threat-feed results together with an aged registration or an inspected page.
      Unable to Determine
      Nothing alarming was found, but there is no positive evidence either, or the destination could not be examined. “Nothing found” is never reported as “safe”.

      Confidence

      Confidence describes how far the verdict can be relied on: how many independent sources were available and how close the score is to a boundary. A high-confidence “Likely Safe” and a low-confidence one are different results, and each report lists exactly which checks could not be run.

      Known limits

      • Pages are fetched as plain HTML. Scripts are never run, so content built in the browser, CAPTCHA walls and pages that show scanners something different are not seen.
      • A legitimate website that has been hacked can host phishing without any tell-tale sign in its address.
      • Message analysis understands English wording only; other languages rely on the link and sender checks.
      • A brand-new phishing domain with an innocuous name and no feed listing will be “Unable to Determine”, not flagged.

      Spotting phishing yourself

      Tools help, but the checks below take seconds and catch most attempts.

      1. Read the address from right to left

      Only the part just before the first single slash matters. In paypal.com.account-check.info/login the site is account-check.info; “paypal.com” is decoration.

      2. A padlock is not a seal of approval

      HTTPS means the connection is encrypted. Most phishing sites have valid certificates because they are free and automatic.

      3. Notice manufactured pressure

      “Your account will be closed in 24 hours”, an unexpected parcel fee, a boss who needs gift cards right now. Urgency plus a request is the pattern to distrust.

      4. Check who is really writing

      The display name is free text. Look at the address after the @. Banks do not write from Gmail, and replies should go back to the same organisation.

      5. Go there yourself

      If a message says there is a problem with an account, open the app or type the address you already know. Never sign in through a link in a message you did not expect.

      6. If you clicked or typed something

      Change that password on the real site and anywhere else you reuse it, turn on two-step verification, and call your bank if payment details were involved. Then report the message.
